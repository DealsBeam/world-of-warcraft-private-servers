When Turtle WoW went offline on May 15, 2026, it took one of the largest Vanilla+ communities with it — roughly eight years of custom content, a playerbase in the tens of thousands, and the most ambitious fan expansion Blizzard's lawyers had ever targeted. Three months later, the scene it left behind is not smaller. It is splintered, busy, and growing: server trackers have logged around twenty new World of Warcraft private servers in the past thirty days alone, many of them explicitly pitched at displaced Turtle players.

Here is where the community actually went, and what the wave of successors means for anyone picking a server today.

## The source leak changed everything

A week after the shutdown, [Turtle WoW's 1.17.1/1.17.2 source leaked](/news/turtle-wow-source-leak/) — the custom core that powered its new zones, races, and quests. That single event transformed the successor landscape. Before the leak, a Turtle-style server meant years of development from scratch. After it, any competent team could fork the core and iterate.

The result is a family tree of projects now tracked on our [homepage](/):

- **[RavenCraft](/servers/ravencraft/)** — built by former Turtle project contributors on the original 1.18.1 core, launching August 22. The closest thing to a direct continuation, and the most anticipated launch of the wave.
- **[Caverns of Time](/servers/caverns-of-time/)** — a Turtle+ project that launched August 15 and already carries an active population.
- **[Capybara WoW](/servers/capybara-wow/)** — run by ex-Turtle SEA admins, two realms, reportedly around 10k online at peak. The largest concentration of former Turtle players right now.
- **[Octo WoW](/servers/octo-wow/)** — restored the 1.18.1 core in July 2026 with three realms (PvE, Hardcore, PvP) and phased raids planned through 2027.

## Not everyone rebuilt on Turtle's bones

The exodus also flowed to projects with entirely different foundations. [Moonwhisper Games](/news/moonwhisper-games/) — the studio formed by ex-Turtle developers in June — is building an original MMORPG on owned IP, having stripped all emulator content from their community channels. Others landed on stock 1.12.1 blizzlike servers, WotLK cores, or Classic+ experiments like [Classic Plus - Ashbringer](/servers/classic-plus-ashbringer/).

That split matters. A fork of Turtle's leaked core inherits its content but also its legal profile: it is, bluntly, Blizzard's code with someone else's additions. Projects on independent cores or open-source emulators occupy a different risk tier — smaller targets, less to seize.

## Picking among the successors

Three things separate the durable projects from the churn:

1. **Team track record.** Capybara's ex-Turtle admins and RavenCraft's former contributors have shipped content before. Anonymous teams forking a leaked core last week have not.
2. **Monetization.** Every major takedown in the past year — [Turtle WoW](/news/turtle-wow-shutdown/), Stormforge, the ongoing Ascension case — involved servers that accepted money. Donation-funded successors are repeating the pattern that got their predecessor sued.
3. **Population trajectory**, not snapshots. A fresh server listing 200 players can double or die in a month; check whether the community is growing before investing leveling time.

## The irony Blizzard built

The enforcement campaign that ended Turtle WoW did not shrink demand for Vanilla+ — it proved it. Twenty new servers in thirty days is the market's answer to a product gap, and every one of them exists because Blizzard's own Classic roadmap left that space empty. Whether Project Camelot fills it after BlizzCon decides how long the wave lasts. Until then, the successors are competing hard for the throne — and players are the beneficiaries.
