For two decades, Blizzard's approach to private servers was mostly cease-and-desist letters and targeted lawsuits against the biggest projects — [Felmyst got a C&D within five hours of launching in 2017](/news/project-epoch-cease-and-desist/). 2026 was different. The strategy escalated to federal lawsuits, and for the first time, RICO — the organized-crime statute — became the legal weapon of choice against emulator operators.

This is the timeline, pulled together from the events we track.

## May 14, 2026 — Stormforge shuts down

Stormforge, the landmark merger of the Tauri and Atlantiss teams, shut down following direct legal intervention. The project had been running for over four years across MoP (Mistblade) and WotLK (Wrath) realms, and was widely respected for its scripting quality. [Full event](/servers/stormforge-mistblade/).

## May 15, 2026 — Turtle WoW shuts down

The bigger blow. Turtle WoW, the custom Vanilla+ server that had run for nearly eight years, closed after losing a federal copyright infringement lawsuit in California — *Blizzard Entertainment, Inc. v. Turtle WoW*. The case had been running through the courts, and after Blizzard won a ruling in April 2026, Turtle agreed to a binding permanent injunction and permanently closed its servers. [Full story](/blog/turtle-wow-shutdown-explained/).

What made the Turtle case notable was how Blizzard prosecuted it: the complaint leveraged the **RICO Act** against individual developers and community managers, and Blizzard aggressively subpoenaed Discord, hosting providers, and payment processors to build the case. That combination — RICO plus going after the infrastructure, not just the server host — became the template for what followed.

## June 2026 — Ascension hit with a RICO lawsuit

Ascension, the classless-WoW project that claimed over a million registered players, had a federal lawsuit filed against its founder and team. The complaint again used RICO allegations over the project's custom launcher and infrastructure. Unlike Turtle and Stormforge, Ascension has kept operating while the case moves through federal court. [News item](/news/blizzard-rico-ascension/).

## What changed

Three shifts mark 2026 apart from every earlier crackdown:

1. **Federal lawsuits over C&D letters.** The Felmyst-style quick cease-and-desist still happens, but the biggest projects now face full federal litigation.
2. **RICO as a weapon.** Aligning emulation projects with organized-crime statutes is a novel legal theory — one that raises the stakes enormously for operators, since RICO carries severe penalties and lets Blizzard go after individuals.
3. **Attacking the infrastructure.** Subpoenas to Discord, hosts, and payment processors make it harder for a project to just relocate. The tools a community depends on become legal pressure points.

## What it means for players

The practical impact is simple: **the biggest servers are now the most exposed.** Being popular puts you in Blizzard's crosshairs. The [Turtle WoW shutdown](/blog/turtle-wow-shutdown-explained/) is the clearest example — it was the most popular custom Classic+ realm, and it's gone.

For players, that means:

- **Don't invest months in a server without checking its health.** Big population + public profile = legal risk.
- **The 2024 Turtle source leak created alternatives.** When Turtle died, [Octo WoW](/servers/octo-wow/), [Capybara WoW](/servers/capybara-wow/), and others rose from its code — the scene adapts even when a flagship falls.
- **New realms are still launching.** The tracker's [in-development list](/) currently includes RavenCraft (launching August 22), Whitemane's Frostmourne (September 9), and Perseus (August 28), among others.

Private servers have survived legal pressure since 2004 — the first major crackdown happened before the game even launched. What changed in 2026 is the playbook. Whether RICO survives the courts will shape whether the scene's biggest projects can keep operating, or whether private servers get pushed back toward smaller, quieter realms.
